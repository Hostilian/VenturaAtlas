# 3. IMMUTABLE RESEARCH CONSTITUTION

Even before the exact original prompt is extracted, the following principles are mandatory and must remain operational.

## 3.1 Capital constraint

The initial business-search objective assumes:

* $0 startup cost is preferred.
* $100 is the absolute maximum before receiving customer revenue.
* No investors.
* No employees.
* No inventory.
* No meaningful savings.
* Customer payment should occur before meaningful expenditure whenever possible.

The $100 ceiling includes every required pre-revenue expense, including:

* Domains.
* Hosting.
* Databases.
* Storage.
* Email.
* AI model usage.
* API usage.
* Automation tools.
* Subscriptions.
* Marketplace fees.
* Developer accounts.
* Payment setup.
* Payment processing.
* Advertising.
* Data.
* Samples.
* Materials.
* Packaging.
* Shipping.
* Refund reserves.
* Legal or company setup.
* Contractors.
* Equipment.
* Contingency.
* Any other genuinely required cost.

A required cost may not be renamed “optional” merely to preserve eligibility.

Ideas that exceed the limit may remain in the archive, but must be labeled ineligible under the original constitution.

## 3.2 Minimum investigation

A complete full research run should document at least:

* 12 distinct research rounds.
* 60 meaningfully different search queries.
* 50 useful sources opened and evaluated.
* 15 primary or authoritative sources.
* 15 skeptical, negative, or contradictory sources.
* 20 meaningfully different markets.
* 60 specific business opportunities.
* 20 opportunities evaluated beyond a one-sentence description.
* 12 opportunities individually cost-checked.
* 10 opportunities compared with a standardized scorecard.
* Five opportunities deeply investigated.
* Three finalists financially modeled and stress-tested.
* One final winner selected when the evidence supports selection.

Do not count:

* Search-result snippets as inspected sources.
* Repeated articles copying the same claim as independent evidence.
* Queries that differ only by trivial wording.
* Long prose as evidence that research occurred.
* Unopened links as evaluated sources.
* Likes, compliments, waitlists, views, or survey interest as payment evidence.

If the minimum requirements are not completed, label the run incomplete and identify the missing work.

## 3.3 Research-plan approval gate

For a brand-new full internet research run:

1. First propose the 12-round research plan.
2. Explain evidence requirements.
3. Explain elimination rules.
4. Explain cost verification.
5. Publish scoring weights.
6. Explain financial stress tests.
7. Explain final verification.
8. Wait for an explicit approval command.

The historical approval wording must be preserved.

Suggested future approval command:

`APPROVE — begin the 12-round investigation.`

This approval gate applies to **new external research runs**.

It does not prevent immediate extraction, normalization, repository construction, testing, archiving, or collaborative-system development from the supplied corpus.

## 3.4 Research allocation

For a full research run, target approximately:

* 75% evidence gathering and source inspection.
* 15% comparison, calculation, contradiction search, and red-team analysis.
* 10% final writing.

Do not choose an early favorite and then mainly search for supporting evidence.

## 3.5 Twelve research rounds

### Round 1 — Structural-change scan

Investigate important changes from the previous 24 months involving:

* Regulation.
* Laws.
* Platform policies.
* APIs.
* AI capabilities.
* Open-source software.
* Accessibility.
* Privacy.
* Cybersecurity.
* Ecommerce.
* Payments.
* Education.
* Scientific workflows.
* Labor shortages.
* Cross-border trade.
* Digital media.
* Search behavior.
* Hardware.
* Climate adaptation.
* Demographics.
* Consumer behavior.

Find at least 30 developments that may have created new spending or new urgent problems.

Do not recommend businesses yet.

### Round 2 — Market diversification

Investigate at least 20 meaningfully different markets.

Do not submit 20 variations of generic SaaS.

Include a mixture of:

* Consumers.
* Prosumers.
* Developers.
* Independent professionals.
* Ecommerce operators.
* Researchers.
* Educators.
* Students.
* Creators.
* Marketplaces.
* Local businesses.
* Data products.
* Monitoring.
* Verification.
* Accessibility.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Localization.
* Licensing.
* APIs.
* Emerging platforms.
* Transactional products.

For each market, record:

* Recent change.
* Existing spending.
* Unresolved problem.
* Current alternatives.
* Accessible distribution.
* Main rejection argument.

### Round 3 — Direct problem discovery

Search direct evidence such as:

* Negative reviews.
* Reddit discussions.
* Professional forums.
* Support forums.
* GitHub issues.
* Feature requests.
* Job listings.
* Freelance listings.
* Manual spreadsheet workflows.
* Administrative guides.
* Migration discussions.
* Pricing complaints.
* Missing integrations.
* Reliability complaints.

Find at least 50 recurring problems.

Classify each problem:

1. Discussed only.
2. Costs time.
3. Costs money.
4. Users already pay.
5. Users urgently seek a replacement.

Prioritize levels four and five.

### Round 4 — Evidence-based opportunity generation

Generate at least 60 specific opportunities based on evidence from earlier rounds.

Every opportunity should be expressible as:

> A product for [specific user] that solves [specific recurring problem] through [specific mechanism], produces [specific valuable output], charges through [specific payment model], reaches customers through [specific low-cost channel], and can be validated for [specific cash amount].

For each opportunity, record:

* Exact user.
* Economic buyer.
* Problem.
* Existing workaround.
* Proposed product.
* Initial price.
* Payment model.
* Distribution route.
* Pre-revenue cash requirement.
* Time to payment test.
* Supporting evidence.
* Strongest failure argument.

Do not manufacture trivial variants merely to reach the quota.

### Round 5 — Spending verification

For the strongest 30 opportunities, determine:

* Whether customers already spend money or labor.
* What they currently buy.
* Who owns the budget.
* Current prices.
* Purchase frequency.
* Cost of doing nothing.
* Adequacy of free alternatives.
* Whether companies hire workers or contractors for the task.
* Whether users switch providers.
* Whether internal scripts or manual processes already exist.

Ideas without credible spending evidence should usually be eliminated unless they have an unusually strong transaction-based model.

### Round 6 — Complete cost audit

For at least the strongest 20, and no fewer than 12 individually verified candidates, calculate all pre-revenue expenses.

For every cost, record:

* Current verified price.
* Official source.
* Verification date.
* Whether mandatory.
* Available free alternative.
* Limitation of the free alternative.
* Date or usage level at which payment becomes necessary.
* Whether customer payment can fund the expense.
* Minimum usage.
* Expected early usage.
* Pessimistic usage.
* Cost per customer or transaction.

Apply a 20% contingency reserve to uncertain costs.

Eliminate from original-constitution eligibility any idea that genuinely requires more than $100 before customer payment.

### Round 7 — Competitor and substitute mapping

For the strongest 15, investigate:

* Direct competitors.
* Indirect competitors.
* Free tools.
* Open-source projects.
* Platform-native features.
* Manual workflows.
* Spreadsheets.
* Freelancers.
* Agencies.
* General-purpose AI.
* Failed competitors.
* Discontinued competitors.

Ask:

* Why would a customer trust a new entrant?
* Can the advantage be copied?
* Are users satisfied with manual work?
* How difficult is switching?
* Is the market dominated by bundled products?
* Is the idea a business or merely a feature?
* Does a free substitute already solve the problem adequately?

### Round 8 — Failure and contradiction search

Deliberately search for evidence that every surviving candidate is a bad business.

Investigate:

* Why customers would not pay.
* Why users cancel.
* Failed startups.
* Customer acquisition difficulty.
* Low margins.
* Hidden costs.
* High support burden.
* Legal restrictions.
* API restrictions.
* Platform-policy risk.
* Refund exposure.
* Fraud.
* Chargebacks.
* Shipping problems.
* AI commoditization.
* Open-source competition.
* Strong free alternatives.

Every finalist must have at least three substantive pieces of negative evidence.

Eliminate ideas that work only when pessimistic evidence is ignored.

### Round 9 — Free distribution investigation

For the strongest 12, determine how to reach:

* The first paying customer.
* Customers two through ten.
* Customers eleven through one hundred.

Investigate:

* Marketplaces with active purchase intent.
* Integration directories.
* Plugin directories.
* Open-source ecosystems.
* Template libraries.
* Professional communities.
* Public diagnostics.
* Public reports.
* Product-led invitations.
* Embedded attribution.
* Collaborative workflows.
* Supplier partnerships.
* High-intent directories.
* Existing search demand.
* Direct outreach to identifiable buyers.

Reject or weaken ideas whose entire distribution strategy is merely:

* “Post on social media.”
* “Do SEO.”
* “Cold email everyone.”
* “Build a community.”
* “Go viral.”

### Round 10 — Payment-first validation

Design a real payment test for the strongest ten.

Each test should:

* Cost no more than $20.
* Run within seven days where reasonably possible.
* Request payment, a deposit, preorder, paid pilot, or paid access.
* Avoid building the complete product.
* Target an exact buyer.
* Promise a concrete deliverable.
* Use a specific recruitment channel.
* Define founder hours.
* Define cash expenditure.
* Define required payments.
* Define pass threshold.
* Define failure threshold.
* Define stop condition.

Waitlists, survey responses, compliments, views, and likes do not count as payment evidence.

### Round 11 — Financial and resilience modeling

For the top five, create bottom-up financial models covering:

* Price.
* Conversion.
* Customers.
* Churn.
* Refunds.
* Payment fees.
* Hosting.
* AI costs.
* API costs.
* Support time.
* Fulfillment.
* Shipping.
* Founder hours.
* Gross profit.
* Operating profit.
* Effective hourly profit.

Model at least:

1. Failure.
2. Tiny success.
3. Survival.
4. Strong success.
5. Exceptional success.

Stress-test:

* Conversion falls 50%.
* Development takes twice as long.
* Price falls 30%.
* Support takes three times longer.
* A free competitor appears.
* A critical free plan disappears.
* API expenses double.
* The main distribution channel underperforms.
* The market is half the estimate.
* Founder availability is limited to 20 hours per week.

The top three must receive expanded comparison.

### Round 12 — Discovery reset and final verification

Do not automatically finalize the current leader.

Generate at least 15 late-stage alternatives by testing:

* Simpler versions.
* Better-paying segments.
* Geographic niches.
* Language niches.
* Transactional versions.
* Data-product versions.
* Licensing versions.
* Bring-your-own-API-key versions.
* Customer-funded fulfillment.
* Adjacent products using the same assets.
* More defensible distribution systems.

Score them under the same framework.

Allow a late-stage candidate to defeat the previous leader.

Immediately before final selection, reverify:

* Current prices.
* Current platform policies.
* Free-tier limitations.
* Commercial-use permissions.
* API availability.
* Competitor activity.
* Legal restrictions.
* Payment costs.
* Refund costs.
* Shipping costs.
* Marketplace fees.

## 3.6 Survival requirements

An idea normally must have all of the following to reach the top 20:

* Two independent indications of recurring pain.
* Evidence that customers spend money or labor.
* A clearly identifiable buyer.
* A deliverable sellable before full automation.
* A credible route to the first customer without paid advertising.
* A verified path to launch below $100.
* No obvious copyright, scraping, licensing, legal, or platform violation.
* A plausible seven-day payment test.
* A path from manual delivery to a repeatable product.
* A reason customers would choose it over free general-purpose AI.

## 3.7 Elimination rules

Retain eliminated ideas in the archive, but mark them eliminated when they:

* Require more than $100 before first payment.
* Require inventory before payment.
* Depend on paid advertising for initial validation.
* Have no identifiable buyer.
* Have no credible spending evidence.
* Depend mainly on virality.
* Require unauthorized scraping.
* Require copyright infringement.
* Violate platform or API rules.
* Carry unacceptable regulatory or liability exposure.
* Have margins too low for support and refunds.
* Have no plausible route to a first customer.
* Are generic freelance labor with no productization path.
* Depend completely on one fragile platform.
* Are adequately replaced by a free tool.
* Require unavailable credentials, licenses, capital, or trust.
* Fail under reasonable pessimistic assumptions.

Each elimination must retain:

* Evidence.
* Date.
* Research run.
* Responsible evaluator.
* Reason.
* Conditions that could reverse the elimination.
