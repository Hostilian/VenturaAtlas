# Executive conclusion

The strongest genuinely different opportunity is **Fit-First Parametric Repair Studio**.

A customer with a broken, low-risk plastic part does not primarily need a beautiful 3D model. The customer needs a file that fits the real object, on the real printer or print service, without paying for several failed full prints.

The proposed workflow is:

1. classify the part and reject unsafe uses;
2. guide the customer through only the measurements that control fit;
3. generate a tiny, cheap fit coupon;
4. test the coupon on the chosen printer and material;
5. record the dimensional correction;
6. generate the final editable STL/STEP/3MF;
7. provide a versioned evidence card and one controlled revision.

The business is **not** generic AI CAD, a custom-printing agency, or an STL marketplace. Deterministic parametric templates create the geometry; AI may classify the archetype, explain measurements, and flag uncertainty, but it should not invent safety-critical geometry.

The provisional offer is **$12–$24 per repair file**, with a printer/shop licence after repeated demand.

# Round 1 — Structural-change scan

- EU right-to-repair rules create stronger incentives and legal support for repair. The directive specifically limits manufacturer practices that impede compliant compatible or 3D-printed parts used by independent repairers.
- Repair Café data repeatedly identifies replacement of parts as a common successful procedure and unavailable or expensive spare parts as a leading cause of failed repairs.
- Academic repair research describes digitizing the missing part and validating print quality as difficult stages rather than trivial printing tasks.
- A study of 41,874 repair records estimated that a meaningful subset of currently unsuccessful repairs might be addressable with 3D-printed parts, while warning that repair descriptions are often too incomplete to identify the correct part and functional requirements.
- Manufacturer-backed initiatives such as Philips Fixables demonstrate that printable parts can become an accepted repair channel, but also show that materials, orientation, testing, and quality limits matter.
- Browser-based text-to-CAD and photo-to-part tools have become abundant, making generic generation a weak differentiation.
- Large public repositories and paid model marketplaces contain millions of files, but exact household replacements are highly fragmented by model, revision, dimensions, and printer behavior.
- Professional custom design can cost tens or hundreds of euros even when the final print itself costs only a few euros.
- FDM prints vary with machine, material, orientation, geometry, and calibration; sources repeatedly recommend small fit coupons before committing to a final functional print.
- Legal and product-safety analysis warns that sellers or manufacturers of replacement components can carry liability. This makes aggressive category exclusion a core product feature.
- The strongest low-capital entry is a digital artifact and validation workflow—not owning printers, shipping inventory, or becoming a full engineering consultancy.

# Round 3 — Direct problem discovery

- **P-01:** The original spare is discontinued, unavailable separately, or more expensive than the object is worth.
- **P-02:** A free repository may not contain the exact model revision or dimensions.
- **P-03:** Non-CAD users cannot reliably convert a broken fragment and mating area into precise geometry.
- **P-04:** A photo alone hides fit-critical dimensions, tabs, offsets, clearances, and material behavior.
- **P-05:** The final printed dimension differs from the nominal CAD dimension.
- **P-06:** A full print may fail after hours because one hole or shaft fit is wrong.
- **P-07:** Customers undervalue modeling, measurement, iteration, and failed tests because the plastic is cheap.
- **P-08:** Print shops receive incomplete photos and dimensions, creating quote delay and revisions.
- **P-09:** Repair groups record product and failure information inconsistently.
- **P-10:** One printer/material combination may fit while another does not.
- **P-11:** One-shot AI CAD can generate plausible but mechanically wrong geometry.
- **P-12:** Users do not reliably recognize when a part is safety-critical.
- **P-13:** Material choice can fail under heat, UV, chemicals, flex, repeated load, or moisture.
- **P-14:** A user may accidentally reproduce protected logos or patented geometry.
- **P-15:** A repair business loses the corrected file and measurements after completing one successful repair.
- **P-16:** A school or makerspace lacks a safe, repeatable way to teach functional repair design.
- **P-17:** Generic craft, sports, gardening, meal, acoustic, and woodworking products are already served by numerous apps.
- **P-18:** A low-frequency consumer repair does not naturally create subscription retention.
- **P-19:** Shipping a physical print introduces geography, tax, returns, and inventory complexity.
- **P-20:** The customer may prefer to replace a low-value object rather than measure and test a part.

# Round 5 — Spending verification

The investigation found four credible forms of existing expenditure:

1. **Custom design:** published examples price simple adapters around €30–€50 and replacement parts based on drawings around €50–€100; another store lists a $135 design fee.
2. **Printing:** small technical parts may cost only a few euros to manufacture once a correct file exists.
3. **Repair:** willingness-to-pay research and repair-industry evidence show that consumers will pay meaningful amounts when repair extends the life of a valuable product, although stated willingness is not equivalent to actual purchase.
4. **Paid files:** large digital-model platforms demonstrate that users buy printable designs, while piracy, free alternatives, and unstable creator income remain substantial.

The pricing wedge is therefore not "cheap plastic." It is reducing a €50–€135 custom-design task to a standardized $12–$24 workflow for a narrow set of archetypes.

# Deep Investigation — Fit-First Parametric Repair Studio

## Customer

- Person with a discontinued or unavailable low-risk plastic part.
- Independent repairer or print shop that repeatedly receives incomplete requests.
- Repair café volunteer who lacks CAD time.

**The customer pays because** a $12–$24 validated file can be cheaper than €50–€135 custom modeling, repeated full-size print failures, or replacing the entire product.

**The idea satisfies the customer by** converting an uncertain reverse-engineering job into a small sequence of measurable, inexpensive decisions.

## Product definition

The user selects a part archetype, photographs the broken part and mating area next to a supplied scale card, enters dimensions, states the environment and failure mode, and chooses a printer/material or print service. The system creates the smallest possible geometry containing the critical fit. After the user tests that coupon, the system applies the observed correction to the final part.

## What future AI should build

- **Frontend:** mobile-first capture flow, WebGL preview, accessible measurement forms, uncertainty display.
- **Geometry:** deterministic OpenSCAD/CadQuery/Manifold templates; editable source retained.
- **AI:** archetype classification, measurement explanation, missing-view detection, risk triage, natural-language report. AI must not silently change dimensions.
- **Core entities:** customer, project, archetype, measurement, photo, mating interface, risk classification, printer profile, material profile, coupon, fit result, model version, licence, shop.
- **Endpoints:** create project, upload evidence, classify archetype, validate measurements, generate coupon, submit fit result, generate final model, request revision, export evidence card.
- **Guardrails:** blocked-category rules, human review triggers, IP declaration, material/environment limits, no safety certification.
- **Analytics:** intake completion, rejected category, coupon download, coupon test, fit success, revision count, final print, refund, replacement value saved.
- **Failure handling:** preserve every version; never overwrite dimensions; allow "cannot model safely"; refund when the archetype is unsuitable.
- **First prototype:** serverless form plus manually executed OpenSCAD templates—no AI model required.

### Break-even example

`Monthly break-even files = fixed costs ÷ (average file revenue − variable cost)`

At $250 fixed cost, $19 average price, and $3 variable/support cost: `250 ÷ 16 = 15.6`, or roughly **16 file-equivalents per month**.
